As syntropic agroforestry practitioners, we see natural cycles at work in our fields every day: the fast succession of our plots, life across every stratum, and the resilience of our target crops. A recent research presentation, the result of a collaboration between [Tzu Chi University’s USR Center](https://usr.tcu.edu.tw/) and the [World Vegetable Center](https://www.worldveg.org/) (WorldVeg), now gives measured evidence of why these systems work so well beneath the surface.

![Gengfutian under an evening sky: young fruit trees on grass on the left, a denser syntropic system with banana on the right|Gengfutian: control (left) and syntropic system (right).](img/blog/blog-res-1.jpg)

The trials took place at Gengfutian, our syntropic demonstration site in Hualien, Taiwan: a 1,000 m² soursop orchard converted to syntropic agroforestry, next to a 1,000 m² control. The study compared the germination, growth and health of pepper plants in syntropic soil, in the adjacent monoculture and in a conventional field in Tainan. The early results point to the role of a healthy soil food web.

![Slide: experimental setup of the Hualien field trial, with photos of the monoculture and syntropic beds and how each was managed|Experimental setup of the field trial. Slide: WorldVeg.](img/blog/blog-res-2.jpg)

## The Fungal Engine

The data show that our biomass management, with chop-and-drop pruning, woodchips and ground cover, changes soil chemistry and biology considerably.

The syntropic soil held 29% organic carbon, against 1.8% in the Hualien monoculture and 0.86% in the conventional field in Tainan. Its microbial biomass was nearly eight times that of the Hualien monoculture, and the community was strongly fungal-dominated, with a fungus-to-bacteria ratio of 3.8:1 against 0.2:1. A diverse microbial community of this kind allows plants to cultivate microbes around their roots and to draw on the interactions they depend on.

## Natural Immunity and Biocontrol

A persistent challenge in growing a target crop such as pepper is soil-borne disease, above all Fusarium wilt. The researchers therefore isolated microbial strains from the soils in search of natural biocontrol agents.

![Slide: Fusarium wilt incidence after applying biocontrol candidates in syntropic and monoculture soil, with photos of seedlings and their roots|Disease incidence after applying biocontrol candidates. Slide: WorldVeg.](img/blog/blog-res-4.jpg)

The syntropic soil yielded nearly twice as many bacterial and fungal strains as the Tainan monoculture soil, and about 80% of the most promising disease-suppressing microbes came from the syntropic plots. In greenhouse tests with soil deliberately infested with Fusarium, plants in syntropic soil showed no wilt, grew larger and formed thicker root systems. The diverse soil community does not only feed the plant; it also protects it, keeping potential pathogens in balance within the ecosystem.

![Slide: pepper plants in syntropic soil with and without Fusarium. With Fusarium: 9 cm tall, 11 cm wide, 14 leaves; without: 6.5 cm, 7.3 cm, 10 leaves; no disease in either. The infested plants have thicker roots|Syntropic soil with and without Fusarium. Slide: WorldVeg.](img/blog/blog-res-5.jpg)

## Field Results: Vigour and Yield

Above ground, the results mirror the soil. Four weeks after transplanting, plants in the syntropic plots were 46% taller and more than 50% wider than in the Hualien control, with about twice the vigour score.

The harvest followed the same pattern. From July to October 2026, the three syntropic plots gave 3.14 kg of pepper against 0.60 kg in the three control plots, without irrigation after March and without fertiliser or sprays. With three plots each, this is a strong trend rather than a proven difference. The syntropic plots also produce soursop, taro, edible fern, cocoa, cassava and herbs, so the pepper is only part of their yield.

This research supports what we see in the field: when our practices follow succession and natural cycles, we do more than grow food. We regenerate the soil, build resilient ecosystems and harvest in abundance.

Many thanks to our colleagues at the World Vegetable Center, and Ilan Henzler, who led the study as a WorldVeg intern, for their careful work, and for their willingness to study these complex systems, with their many interactions, in a first set of trials. We hope there will be more to come.
