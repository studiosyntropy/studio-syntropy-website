When we talk about syntropic agroforestry, we often focus on what happens above ground: the rapid growth of biomass, the use of every stratum, and eventually the harvest. Yet much of the work takes place beneath our feet, in the soil and its living cycles.

In September 2025, during Typhoon Ragasa, a barrier lake formed by an earlier typhoon overflowed. About 60 million tonnes of water and sediment swept through central Guangfu, Hualien, Taiwan, burying fields under silt and severely damaging their soil life.

[Tzu Chi University joined the recovery effort.](https://usr.tcu.edu.tw/?p=1438)

Since then, Prof. Chen Jun-Yao (陳俊堯) of Tzu Chi University has analysed how the soil bacterial community at our field sites is recovering. His data indicate that active management can speed up this recovery.

## The Baseline: What Healthy Soil Looks Like

Our main reference is GFT (Gengfutian, 耕福田), sampled on 27 November 2025. The 2,000 m² site was a nine-year-old soursop orchard on very rocky soil. Thirteen months before sampling, half of it (1,000 m²) was converted into a diverse syntropic agroforestry system.

![Syntropic tree lines with banana and fruit trees beside a lawn at Gengfutian, university buildings behind|GFT, Gengfutian: the main reference site.](img/blog/blog-guf-2.jpg)

All 15 of the most abundant bacterial genera at GFT, among them Bacillus, Sphingobium and Arenimonas, made up at least 1% of the community each. We take this as a picture of a functioning soil microbiome, supported by the active roots of a diverse planting. As a second reference, we sampled LS (Luoshan, 羅山), a site not affected by the flood, on 4 February 2026.

## After the Flood: BZ and LT

We compared these references with two flood-affected sites: BZ (Pangcah Organic Farm) and LT (a private open field). Both were sampled on 25 November 2025, two months after the flood had left them bare and, in places, under up to a metre of silt.

The two sites differed mainly in the level of human intervention:

BZ received substantial organic inputs, applied at irregular intervals, through the implementation of a syntropic agroforestry design.

![Grey flood silt across the field at BZ, with dark patches of organic material and young plantings, mountains behind|BZ: organic inputs on the silt.](img/blog/blog-guf-3.jpg)

LT received few organic inputs and was sown with a grass and legume seed mix.

## Succession in Action: The Effect of Organic Inputs

Soil life recovers in stages, and the data reflect this:

- Pioneer microbes colonise bare post-flood soil quickly, while others take much longer to return.
- At BZ, seven of the 15 dominant GFT genera had already re-established at 1% or more of the community.
- At LT, only one had: Exiguobacterium, a stress-tolerant genus.

## Why Intervention Matters

For practitioners, the message is practical, and especially relevant as climate extremes become more frequent.

Organic inputs and active management, even when applied irregularly as at BZ, supported a partial recovery of the soil community within two months. Where inputs were low, as at LT, recovery was considerably slower.

When we manage a system actively, we are not only planting, pruning and cutting grass; we are feeding the soil food web. A diverse microbial community supports plant health and resilience, and the plants in turn rebuild soil structure and habitat for further life. This reciprocal cycle is the engine of syntropy.

To follow this succession, we will compare these results with a new set of samples taken on 15 April 2026, and share how the soil community developed over the spring.
